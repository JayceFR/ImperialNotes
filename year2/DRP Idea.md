That's actually a much stronger concept than a simple button press because it transforms the interaction from **sending a signal** into **maintaining a relationship**.

What you're describing is essentially a **shared digital garden** that acts as a representation of a relationship and wellbeing.

### Core Theme: Garden of Thoughts

**People**

- Norman (father)
- Sadie (daughter)

**Shared Goal**

- Care for the same virtual plant/tree together.

**Daily Interaction**

- Each person waters the plant once a day.
- Watering can be accompanied by:
    - a short thought
    - a photo
    - a voice note
    - a mood update

The plant grows based on their collective participation.

---

### Why It Feels More Human

The original interaction:

> Norman presses button → Sadie gets notification.

is transactional.

The new interaction:

> Norman contributes to a shared garden that represents his connection with Sadie.

is relational.

The technology becomes almost invisible. The focus is on the relationship rather than the notification.

---

### A Possible Story

Imagine Norman and Sadie live in different cities.

Every day:

- Sadie adds a photo of her morning coffee.
- Norman records a 10-second voice note.
- Their shared tree grows new leaves.

Over time the garden becomes a visual diary of their relationship.

A tree planted six months ago might contain:

- hundreds of photos
- voice notes
- memories
- milestones

This creates emotional attachment.

---

### Escalation Without Feeling Clinical

This is where your idea becomes interesting.

Instead of:

> "Norman has not checked in for 7 days."

you could say:

> "Your oak tree misses Norman's care."

or

> "Sadie watered the garden today. Would you like to add something too?"

or

> "A new leaf is waiting to grow."

The system nudges connection rather than monitoring behaviour.

---

### Mapping Wellbeing to the Garden

You could subtly infer wellbeing through participation.

For example:

|Behaviour|Garden Representation|
|---|---|
|Regular interaction|Tree flourishing|
|Fewer interactions|Leaves becoming sparse|
|Positive mood entries|Flowers blooming|
|Shared memories|New branches|
|Long inactivity|Garden becoming dormant|

The user never sees a "mental health score."

Instead they see a living representation of engagement.

---

### Your Core Interaction Requirement

You still need the Norman → Sadie interaction.

You could define it as:

1. Norman waters the tree.
2. Norman records a voice note.
3. Sadie receives a new leaf on the shared tree.
4. Sadie opens the leaf and hears the recording.
5. Sadie responds by watering the tree and adding a photo.

That is a complete interaction loop involving both people.

---

### Why lecturers often like concepts like this

You can justify it using three HCI principles:

- **Emotional design** — the garden creates attachment.
- **Reciprocity** — both people contribute.
- **Ambient awareness** — Sadie can sense Norman's wellbeing without intrusive monitoring.

So the project stops being:

> "How do we send a notification?"

and becomes:

> "How do we help family members maintain meaningful connections through a shared digital space?"

The danger with the garden idea is that it can accidentally become:

> "WhatsApp, but with plants."

If that's how it comes across, your lecturers will likely ask: _"Why not just use WhatsApp?"_

## Why WhatsApp doesn't fully solve the problem

Suppose Sadie is worried about Norman.

On WhatsApp, she might:

- text him
- call him
- ask if he's okay

If Norman is independent, he may start feeling:

- monitored
- checked up on
- treated like a child

The reassurance comes at the cost of autonomy.

This is exactly the tension your project is trying to address.

---

## Where the Garden Becomes Interesting

The strongest version of your idea isn't:

> "Norman sends messages through a plant."

It's:

> "Norman's normal participation in a shared activity provides reassurance without requiring explicit wellbeing reporting."

The garden becomes a **proxy for presence**, not a messaging app.

For example:

### WhatsApp

Sadie:

> "Dad, are you okay?"

Norman:

> "Yes."

This feels like monitoring.

### Garden

Sadie notices:

- Norman watered the tree today.
- He left a voice note.
- The garden is growing.

Without asking a single question, she knows:

> "Dad is engaging with life today."

That reassurance is gained indirectly.

---

## The Escalation Is Actually the Novel Part

I think this is where your project has the most value.

Imagine three levels:

### Level 1: Normal

Norman interacts with the garden.

Everything is fine.

Sadie doesn't need to do anything.

---

### Level 2: Gentle Nudge

Norman hasn't visited for a few days.

Instead of:

> "Norman failed to check in."

The system says:

> "Sadie added a new flower to your garden."

or

> "A branch is ready to grow together."

The nudge is framed around the shared activity.

---

### Level 3: Family Concern

Norman hasn't interacted for a significantly longer period.

Now Sadie receives:

> "Your garden has been quiet lately."

Rather than:

> "Norman may be at risk."

The language remains respectful.

The escalation focuses on reconnecting people rather than surveilling them.

---

## A More Powerful Framing

I might even avoid presenting it as a wellbeing tracker.

Instead:

> **A shared digital garden that naturally reflects ongoing connection between family members.**

The wellbeing reassurance emerges from that connection.

Because older adults often reject systems that feel like:

- monitoring
- surveillance
- health tracking
- reporting

But they are much more likely to accept:

- sharing memories
- nurturing something together
- leaving little traces of themselves

---

## What I'd Tell a Lecturer

I would say:

> Existing tools like WhatsApp enable direct communication, but they often require family members to actively check on older relatives, which can feel intrusive and undermine independence.
> 
> Our shared garden creates passive reassurance. Family members gain confidence from observing ongoing participation in a meaningful shared activity rather than repeatedly asking wellbeing-related questions.
> 
> Escalation occurs through relationship-centred nudges rather than surveillance alerts, preserving the older adult's autonomy while still providing reassurance.

That distinction is what separates this from "WhatsApp with plants." The value is not messaging; it's **reassurance through shared engagement without making the older person feel monitored**.
