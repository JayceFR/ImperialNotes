# Principles of copyright in software 

### Copyright 
Copyright is a set of legal property rights which the author of a work, or their employer, is granted by a statue. 
- Allows owners to control certain acts in relation to that work. 
- Generate income from their works by acts such as selling copies. 
It can also be used to control the use of works in other ways. 

### Legal basis of Copyright 
- Copyright is a *statutory* right created in UK domestic law by legislation passed by parliament. 
	**Statutory** means, a law that is formally wirtten, debated and enacted by a legislative body. It is the highest form of law in the UK and takes prcednece over judge made common law.
- It is more internationally uniform. 
Three legislations
- Convention for the protection of literary and artistic works 
- 
#### Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artisitc works
also known as the *Berne convention*. 
	This dates back to `1886 `and establishes basic principles as recognition of copyright in works authored overseas. 
	Sets out **international** minimum term of protection of the life of the author + `50` years. 
	So for *example*. 
	If author dies in `2020`. Their work must remain protected until atleast `2070`. 
	- You write software in the UK.
	- Someone in France copies it illegally.
	- France must recognise your copyright because both countries follow the Berne Convention.
- **Directive on the Legal Protection of Computer Programs (the Software Directive)**
	Directive `2009/24/EC` is the current piece of EU legislation extending copyright to computer programmes. It details the key rights of copyright holders, but also extends particulart rights to legal users of software. 
- **Information Society Directive** 
	Directive `2001/29/EC` harmonises copyright more generally. 
	Another exception which was introduced by ISD is the temporary copies were produced for technical reasons. This placed on a more secure legal footing activities such as web caches and the copying of information which occurs automatically as it moves around copmputer systems.

### Copyright as intellectual property. 
Copyright is described as a property. 
**Property rights** are distinguished from other legal rights under, for example, contract law or in tort. 
- Propert rights are enforceable against **any 3rd parties**. So an owner of a property can exclude any third party from his land. True for copyrights as well 
  But in **contracts**, if Alice enters into a contract with Bob, then Charle is unrestricted by any of the points in the contract. So for example, lets say Alice and Bob enter into a contract saying Alice needs to pay Bob $200. Charlie doesn't need to pay any money and wouldn't be sued if he doesn't pay anything as well. 
- Property rights are **alienable**, they can be transferred in whople or parts to other parties. This is true for copyright as well, and the transferee then enjoys the rights on the same bassis as the original holder. 
- Propertyt rights can be **waived** (remvoed) in part by granting of licences. So I can waive my right to sue you for trespass by inviting you onto my land. Similarly the copyright holder may grant licences to copy, distribute or otherwise deal in copyright works. 
Nice!
*Note* Violating copyright can in some cases be a criminal offence, but it will be an offence under the **Copyright Designs and Patents Act 1998** and unrelated to the offence of theft created by the Theft Act 1968. 
### Other forms of intellectual property
**Trademark** Restricts the use by thrid parties of the distincitve names and logos associated with products, including software. 

**Patent** protects the ideas begind inventions. 
*Note:* In Europe Software per se is not patentable but software which causes physical effects may be patentable as a part of an invetions. 
In US, a much broader class of software is patentable. 

**Database rights** are rights in the collection of data together to form a database. 

**Design rights** protect the design of objects from copying. These are not directly relevant to software development but can be relevant to graphical designs implemented in software, for example for fonts. 

**Topography rights** rights in the design of semiconductors. These of relevance to hardware designers. 

### Programs are books 
Copyrights applies to far more than software: books, art work, music and audio and video recordings. 
Computer programs being quite a lot newer than most copyright law, so were slotted in later. Computer programs (in both source and object form) are considered to be literary. 
However as we shall see below recent legislation, particularly the Software Directive, has addressed this somewhat by creating rights and limitaitons which are peculiar to comptuer programmes and do not apply to other literary works. 

### Rights granted by copyright. 
These rights belong exclusively to the copyright holder and subject ot only limited exceptions.
Everyone else requries the copyright holder's permission to do any of these things. 
- The right to make copies whether permanent or temporary, in particular this includes those copies required to load display and run the program. 
- The right to translate, adapt arrange or otherwise alter the program. In particular this includes **compiling**, **decmpliting** and translating into another programming language. 
- The right to distribute to the public including renting copies of the program. 
These rights are laid out in `article 4.1 of the Software Directive, and in sections 16-21 of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988.`

### Exceptions of copyright 
Articles `5` and `6` of the Software Directive lay out several exceptions / limitations to the exlusive rights of the copyright holder.
- **Right to use** In the absense of specific contractual (licence) terms to the contrary, someone with a lawfully acquired copy of a piece of software is entitles to use it for its intended purppose and any copies made in this course of this will not infringe copyright. This even extends to modfying the program to correct errors. 
  So for example lets say we buy a game (legally acquire it). The game would get copied to cache, which is allowed. 
- **Right to make a backup** Someone with the right to use a piece of software is also entitled to make a backup copy where that is necessary for the use. Quite limited right 
- **Right to observe, study and test** If the person has right to use a copy of the computer program, then they can without authoirsation of the right holder can observe, study or test the functioning of the program in order to determine the ideas and princiuples which underlie any element of the program.
  So basically he can use debuggers, network traffic monitoring tools and other monitoring tools. 
- **Right to decompile** In limited circumstances, it is legal to copy and traslate an otherwise legally held copy of a piece of software in order to achieve interoperability with an independently created piece of software. This rigth is restricted to those actions needed to acheive interoperability and the relevant information must not be readily avaiable.

### Clean Room IBM 
IBM did not, however, want other companies to be in the market of making IBM compatible computers themselves. IBM came up with an ingenious strategy: they published the full source code for the BIOS in their documentation, but without a licence which would enable a competitor to legally copy the BIOS code in a new machine. By doing this IBM sought to “poison the well” by ensuring that any developer with the expertise to work on a competing BIOS would have seen the IBM source code and could be accused of copying it. IBM did not hold an exclusive licence to the MS-DOS operating system which shipped with the PC, so a competitor who could legally clone the BIOS would be in a position to build compatible computers and could licence MS DOS from Microsoft to provide a complete system.

**Clean Room**
So there is a dirty room, where there are people who have seen the source code and they write the spec and then hand the spec over to the clean room. Now the clean room people would then implement the code and then hand the code to the dirty room. The dirty room would then modify the spec again and then reiterate. GENIUS!

### Software Licences 
A developer wishing to incorporate proprietary software in his or her work must obtain a licence from the copyright holder to do so and will often need to pay for the privilege. However there is another class of licence in common use which is designed not to extract revenue from copyright, but rather to make the copyright work available to as many people as possible, and to enable those people considerable freedom to do with that software as they please. These licences are known as free software or open source licences, and have their origins in the ideas of an American computer programmer named Richard Stallman (GNU goat).
- **Freedom 0** The freedom to run the program, for any purpose. 
- **Freedom 1** The freedom to study how the program works, and change it so it does your computing as you wish. (Access to the source code is a precondition for this.) 
- **Freedom 2** The freedom to redistribute copies so you can help your neighbor. 
- **Freedom 3** The freedom to distribute copies of your modified versions to others. By doing this you can give the whole community a chance to benefit from your changes. (Access to the source code is a precondition for this.)

### BSD licence 
Redistribution and use in source and binary forms, with or without modification, are permitted provided that the following conditions are met: 
- Redistributions of source code must retain the above copyright notice, this list of conditions and the following disclaimer. 
- Redistributions in binary form must reproduce the above copyright notice, this list of conditions and the following disclaimer in the documentation and/or other materials provided with the distribution.

So basically it lets us do whatever we want. But we just need to keep the header in the licence. So similar to writing a report or something like that, we would need to give credit to them, so cite them. 

The BSD can work with any other licence, this means say Alice wrote a software ans licenced it using BSD. Bob can use Alice's code and make a proprietary software and then sell it for money.

So its like a **virus**. You would need to give them your source code along with the binary. 

### LGPL Licence 
You can use my library in proprietary software, but modifications to the library itself stay free.
So when distributing it in closed source and say they are using some piece of software licenced under LGPL, then they should allow the end user to relink a modifeid version of that LGPL library. So users can modify that library. 
Suppose a company writes:

```
Video Editor Pro
```

and uses an LGPL multimedia library.

The company can:

- Keep Video Editor Pro closed-source.
- Sell it commercially.

But they must:

- Provide the LGPL library source code.
- Allow users to swap in a modified version of that library.

They cannot lock the software so tightly that users are unable to replace the LGPL component.
